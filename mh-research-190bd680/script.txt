9 Invisible Rules Directing Business in Vietnam (And Why Ignoring Them Costs You Dearly)

========================================
PART 1: COLD OPEN: RULE 1 (0:00–1:00)
========================================
[On screen: TEXT: Rule 1 · "Yes" often means "I heard you", not "I agree"]

When a Vietnamese partner says "yes"… do you think that means they agree?

In my first years in Vietnam, I did. And I was disappointed so many times.

Because here, "yes" has many meanings. Very often it doesn't mean "I agree". It means: I understand. I'm open to this.

So I left meetings thinking we had a deal. And then nothing happened, because for the other side there was never an agreement. That's how I lost potential clients who were a perfect fit for my company, and people who would have been great on my team.

Cost: lost clients, lost hires. So when you hear "yes", ask one more question: what's the next step, and when?

That's rule one. And there are eight more that nobody writes down.

PART 2: WELCOME + WHY (1:00–1:30)
========================================
[On screen: CHART: Hofstede · "wanting clear rules" (uncertainty avoidance) Belgium 94 · Vietnam 30 — "I come first" vs "the group comes first" (individualism) Belgium 81 · Vietnam 30. Source: The Culture Factor]

Welcome to another video about what it's like to do business in Vietnam from a Western perspective. My name is Mieke, and I've been living and working here for nine years.

I'm from Belgium. We want clear rules, and the individual comes first. In Vietnam it's almost the opposite: rules are flexible, and the group and the relationship come first. Not better, not worse. Just different. So let's go.

PART 3: RULES 2–3: IN THE MEETING (1:30–3:20)
========================================
[On screen: TEXT: Rule 2 · The real decision-maker is often not in the room]
[On screen: TEXT: Rule 3 · Never correct someone in front of others]

This happened to me many times, and to my Vietnamese staff too.

Someone on the phone tells you: yes, this is my business. Yes, I make the decisions. Yes, I handle the money. And when it's time to decide, they refer you to another person. Someone who was never in the conversation, never even mentioned.

Cost: a lot of wasted time. So early on, ask in a friendly way: who else is part of this decision?

And this is not only with staff. It's with anyone.

People here are scared to lose face. And, most importantly, they don't want you to lose face either. Most of us Westerners don't care about that. We just want to reach the goal as fast and efficiently as possible.

Cost: in my first years, I definitely lost staff members because I didn't know this. So give feedback one-on-one. Calm, and private.

PART 4: RULES 4–6: RELATIONSHIPS, CONTRACTS, PAPERWORK (3:20–6:00)
========================================
[On screen: TEXT: Rule 4 · Relationship first, contract second]
[On screen: TEXT: Rule 5 · Rules and timelines on paper bend in practice]
[On screen: CHART: EuroCham Business Confidence Index, Q2 2026: 53% of surveyed member companies named regulatory delays, policy inconsistency and tax administration as their biggest obstacles to long-term expansion]
[On screen: TEXT: Rule 6 · The right introduction opens doors]
[On screen: CHART: first time · months, over USD 4,000 → next times · much less time and effort ⚠ waiting for Mieke's OK]

We see this all the time at The Marketing House. Vietnamese business owners reach out. They're interested. And then: short messages, back and forth, back and forth.

We like to work fast. Understand the problem, the goal, and explain how we can help. And everything about us is online: our work, our team, our clients, here on YouTube, on Facebook, on our website.

But for many Vietnamese clients, that's not enough. They want to meet for coffee and get to know you first.

Cost: push too fast, and the conversation goes nowhere. So plan for the coffee. It's where the deal really starts.

We have contracts with everyone: suppliers, partners, employees, our landlord, our clients. Our accountant checks them, and when needed our law firm. And still, a signed contract is often not seen as something to commit to one hundred percent. So we explain, patiently: what's on the paper, we all stick to.

And it's not only contracts. In EuroCham's latest survey, 53 percent of the European companies asked named regulatory delays and unclear rules as their biggest obstacle to growing here.

Cost: months of delay. So add buffer time to every plan.

Paperwork is still a challenge, even though I've seen huge improvements in my nine years here.

My own example: my work permit and residence card. The first time, it took months and months, and it cost over four thousand dollars.

The next times, we knew which people to speak to, and it all took much less effort, time and money.

Cost: months, and thousands of dollars. So before any paperwork, ask around: who has done this before?

PART 5: RULES 7–9: DINNERS, TẾT, TITLES (6:00–7:50)
========================================
[On screen: TEXT: Rule 7 · The business dinner is where trust is built]
[On screen: TEXT: Rule 8 · The Tết bonus and gifts are expected, not a nice extra]
[On screen: CHART: Average Tết bonus 2026 — foreign-invested companies 9.21 million VND · all companies 8.69 million VND. Source: Ministry of Home Affairs]
[On screen: TEXT: Rule 9 · Age and titles matter: who speaks first, who sits where]

My Vietnamese friends tell me some business people bring a lot of beer to see how much the Western partner can drink. Their logic: if you're still a good, decent, smart person with all that beer in you, you'll be a good partner too. 🙂

Cost: skip the dinner, and you skip the trust. You don't have to drink like a champion. And if you drive, don't drink: the law here is very strict.

In 2026, foreign-invested companies paid an average Tết bonus of more than nine million dong per employee. I've always given a bonus, and very occasionally gifts. I hope I'm doing a good job there. I still have a lot to learn!

Cost: get it wrong, and your team notices. So put it in your budget from January.

Honestly, after all these years, this is still difficult for me. I don't care at all who sits where or who speaks first. I just want everyone to feel comfortable. So in meetings, I let my Vietnamese partners lead the way, or my Vietnamese staff decide. Then I don't have to worry about it. 🙂
